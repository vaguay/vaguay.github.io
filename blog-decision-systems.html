Ethics Is Goal Selection

Thoughts from Automated Decision Systems and ideas I kept connecting back to my Mind, Brains, and Machines cognitive science class.

Hi guys.

"There exists, according to Jorge Luis Borges, one library that contains every conceivable story in the universe. In “The Library of Babel,” that universe is an infinite library filled with books made from every possible combination of letters and punctuation. In The Drug Hunters: The Improbable Quest to Discover New Medicines, Kirsch and Ogas compare the search for new medicines within a chemical space of roughly 10^60 possibilities to the search for meaning in Borges’s library (Pearce, 2019). Building a universally ethical computer would face the same problem. A system built to account for every possible combination of relevant rules, values, goals, and priority weights might be complete in principle, but it would be unusable in practice. Human beings do not reason by searching a complete moral library. They act within a constrained hypothesis space shaped by biology, culture, and context, selecting some goals as salient while ignoring others. That is the central limit of Goal-Based Decision Making (Slade, 1993). Slade’s framework makes decisions more explicable, but explicability alone is not enough for ethics, because ethical reasoning depends not only on how goals are balanced once selected, but on how certain goals become salient in the first place. An ethical computer, therefore, should not be designed as a universal moral reasoner, but as a contextual, modular goal-based system that makes its value commitments explicit, underscores dissonance, and remains auditable within a bounded domain."

That was the intro for my final paper. A lot of conversations around ethical AI assume the problem is:

“How do we make a machine choose the morally correct answer?”

But after reading Goal-Based Decision Making by Slade and connecting it with cognitive science ideas about salience, inductive biases, startup software, and bounded reasoning, I started thinking the real question is:

How does a system decide what even matters enough to consider in the first place?

The Core Idea

My paper basically argues this:

Ethics is not just decision-making. Ethics is goal selection.

Humans do not reason by searching through every possible moral rule like some infinite library (like the Library of Babel). We notice certain things first. Some goals become salient. Others disappear into the background.

That filtering process happens before explicit reasoning even begins.

Cognitive science already tells us this. Babies are born with attentional biases toward faces and socially relevant information. Humans allocate attention through emotion, memory, fear, culture, relationships, and context long before formal logic enters the picture.

Why This Matters for AI

A lot of AI ethics discussions focus on outputs.

Did the system approve the loan correctly? Did the hiring system discriminate? Did the medical model make the right diagnosis?

But I think ethical failure often happens much earlier.

The ethics are already embedded in what the system was designed to notice.

If a lending model prioritizes formal credit history while ignoring informal family support systems, remittances, or community-based economics, that is not neutral.

If a medical AI is trained mostly on populations that are easiest to measure, what cognitive science calls the “streetlight effect,” then the model is already inheriting a morally loaded picture of reality before it ever makes a prediction.

The system is not just making decisions. It is deciding what counts.

Why I Don’t Think A Universal Ethical AI Works

One of the most interesting realizations I had while writing this paper is that a truly universal ethical AI probably would not even be socially acceptable.

Different institutions, cultures, and people fundamentally disagree on goals.

I used the example of Anthropic refusing certain military uses of AI while the Pentagon wanted fewer restrictions. Neither side was “irrational.” They were operating under different ethical reference frames.

Ethics is not one global coordinate system.

It is competing salience structures and competing goal hierarchies.

The Part I Found Most Interesting

The idea I became most obsessed with was cognitive dissonance.

Most AI systems today try to sound smooth, confident, and coherent. Large language models are optimized to reduce friction and uncertainty in their responses.

But humans do not experience ethical reasoning that way.

Real ethics feels conflicted.

Fairness conflicts with efficiency. Safety conflicts with autonomy. Access conflicts with risk.

So my proposal was that ethical AI systems should not hide dissonance. They should surface it.

Instead of pretending certainty, a system should explicitly say:

  • which goals were prioritized
  • which goals were sacrificed
  • what tradeoffs occurred
  • what alternative priorities would have changed the outcome

An ethical system should not only explain itself. It should explain where it is conflicted.

What I Would Explore Next

If I kept building this idea further, I would probably explore:

  • bounded ethical agents
  • salience-layer architectures
  • AI systems that defer decisions under uncertainty
  • dissonance visualization interfaces
  • goal-hierarchy modeling
  • cross-cultural inductive bias mapping

Honestly, this paper changed how I think about AI entirely.

I no longer think the hardest problem is building a more intelligent system.

I think the hardest problem is deciding what the system should care about in the first place.
